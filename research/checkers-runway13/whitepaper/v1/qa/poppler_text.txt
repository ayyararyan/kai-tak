Executive summary

Kai Tak’s Runway 13 presents a problem in coupled constraints. The approach must deliver an aircraft to
the runway with the right position, direction, height and speed, while leaving enough distance to complete
the landing. A turn that is geometrically possible may demand too much bank or thrust. A trajectory that
reaches the threshold may leave an unacceptable touchdown or stopping condition. Understanding the
approach therefore requires both a dated reconstruction of the setting and a model that continues beyond
runway alignment.
This paper develops the first components of that study. The historical reference is Hong Kong Civil Avia-
tion Department Aircraft Accident Report 1/95, concerning CAL605 on 4 November 1993. Its procedure
and runway information establish a 47-degree difference between the reported magnetic localiser and
runway directions, and a landing distance available of 2,786 metres. The report also preserves selected
flight-recorder plots and weather information. Those records are valuable, but they neither describe a
representative sample of normal landings nor identify a uniquely optimal historical flight path.
The reconstruction combines oﬀicial historical survey sheets, aerial photographs and explicitly dated mod-
ern geospatial products. A local stereo reconstruction from two photographs taken on 2 November 1993
yields 171,307 filtered surface points. The vertical coordinate is relative to an estimated local park/road
plane, not an aviation elevation datum. Only 3,780 points lie within the local map-control hull. This is
useful measured historical geometry with uneven support, not yet a complete obstacle surface.
The mathematical component constructs an explicit approach, flare and ground-stop trajectory in a stipu-
lated point-mass scenario. Turning, descent, speed and force demands are checked together. A retained
computer-assisted certificate supports a minimum normalized constraint margin of at least 0.0397 for one
exact-decimal candidate. In that scenario the aircraft touches down about 313.49 metres beyond the us-
able threshold and stops at 2,531.32 metres, leaving 254.68 metres. These are model calculations with
zero nominal wind and no historical obstacles. Their decimal precision does not describe real-aircraft
accuracy.
A separate necessary bound puts the best score in the same finite trajectory family below 0.1559772.
The gap is too wide to support a claim of near-optimality. Existence of a best member of that family
is established separately from locating it. The paper also derives exact conditional ranges for stopping
distance over a supplied parameter box; no physical parameter box or calibrated prediction interval is
supplied in this version.
Historical weather adds an important diagnostic. The retained routine station reports immediately brack-
eting the accident period record a mean wind of 10.8 metres per second. In a specified quality-filtered
subset of 1993 routine observations, 99.10 percent of records have a lower mean wind speed. This places
the selected accident case near the high-wind end of that observed subset. It is descriptive context, not
an accident probability or a distribution of winds encountered by aircraft.
The selected flight/weather benchmark supplies a first historical confrontation. Substituting the report’s
rounded touchdown inputs into the same illustrative stopping law changes remaining runway from about
255 metres to minus 162 metres. During later report-stated rollout stages, speed falls while the constant
deceleration required to stop rises. These calculations expose the importance of stopping-aid state and
activation history; they do not establish that the synthetic approach was historically flyable. A completed
improvement comparison must hold aircraft, information, environment and objective fixed. No historical
superiority or safer operational alternative is claimed.

1 The research question

The visual drama of an approach is an unreliable guide to its mathematical diﬀiculty. A steep-looking
turn may be compatible with the aircraft’s operating envelope, while a visually gentle path may consume
too much distance or require an unsustainable speed change. At Kai Tak, the more useful question is how
the constraints fit together: where the aircraft can turn, how much height it can lose, what state it must
reach at the runway, and how much runway remains after touchdown.
The phrase Kai Tak paradox is used here as a project framing, not as an established named theorem or
a claim that historical operations violated a physical bound. It refers to the apparent tension between a
constrained-looking approach and the existence of flown trajectories through it. The research task is to
replace that impression with a precisely defined feasible set and a fair comparison.
Let 𝜃 collect aircraft configuration, initial conditions, environment and information assumptions, and let
ℱ(𝜃) denote the admissible trajectories or policies under those assumptions. Three questions follow. Is
ℱ(𝜃) nonempty? Are documented historical observations consistent with the model and its uncertainty?
Does a candidate improve a specified criterion relative to a comparable baseline? These questions require
different evidence. A constructed feasible path answers the first within its model. A measured flight can
challenge the model. A strict, verified improvement over one baseline says nothing by itself about global
optimality.
An entire published procedure should not be treated as one observed curve. Pilots can fly different trajec-
tories consistent with a procedure, with different aircraft states and weather. Similarly, a recorded path
does not identify the pilot’s complete feedback policy: it reveals one response to one realized sequence
of conditions. This distinction will matter when uncertainty is introduced.


1.1 Scope of the first version

This version develops a research benchmark and reports the results that have supporting artifacts. It
uses 1993 as the main historical reference, while retaining other source epochs explicitly. It presents
one synthetic approach-to-stop candidate and its restricted certificate. The historical reconstruction and
flight-evidence analysis have not yet been integrated into that candidate’s constraints.
The distinction is substantive. The synthetic model is useful for testing equations, understanding coupling
and verifying a candidate continuously in time. It is not a substitute for the historical problem. Conversely,
an increasingly detailed reconstruction does not by itself determine aircraft dynamics or identify the best
approach. Both components must eventually meet in a common coordinate system with defensible uncer-
tainty.


2 Historical evidence and the comparison it permits

2.1 The primary historical anchor

CAD Report 1/95 concerns the Boeing 747-409B B-165 operating CAL605 on 4 November 1993 [1].
Sections 1.8–1.10 describe the instrument guidance and runway; Appendices 1, 11 and 12 preserve the
approach chart, selected recorder plots and a reconstructed flight path. The report supplies a coherent
primary reference for the chosen period, rather than a composite of charts from different years.

The reported localiser direction is 088∘ magnetic and the runway direction is 135∘ magnetic. Their dif-

1.7 nautical miles from touchdown. The runway is reported as 3,331.5 metres long and 61 metres wide,

2.2 What the recorder evidence can establish

The report describes a recorder containing 269 parameters, but publishes only selected plots. Those
images are not raw machine-readable flight data. Digitization can recover approximate curves if axis
calibration, trace thickness, overlapping lines and time alignment are handled explicitly. It cannot recover
missing sampling metadata or turn a thick raster line into a high-precision measurement.
Several variables also require careful interpretation. Cockpit control-wheel, control-column and pedal
positions are not automatically aerodynamic surface deflections. Thrust-lever angle was not recorded.
Engine indications therefore cannot be relabelled as directly observed throttle inputs. The Appendix 12
flight path was reconstructed by integrating accelerations; comparing it with those same accelerations is
a consistency check, not independent positional validation.
Most importantly, the report’s discussion of touchdown does not support a causal claim against the ge-
ometry of the approach. Section 2.6.5 describes a controllable touchdown and states that touchdown
parameters were not a causal factor in the accident. The approximate touchdown station and groundspeed
reported there are evidence about that case, not proof that an alternative turn would have prevented the
overrun. This paper treats CAL605 as an informative reconstruction benchmark, not as the historical
optimum or a representative normal flight.

3 Reconstructing the approach area through time

3.1 A dated reconstruction, not a timeless city

The relevant obstacle set is the city at a stated time. A modern polygon with a construction-related record
does not necessarily reproduce its historical footprint, roof or surrounding ground. Demolished structures
are particularly diﬀicult to recover from a database containing only current buildings. Absence from such
a database is not historical absence.
The acquisition assembled 227 valid historical survey-map archives across 93 core sheet locations and 48
full aerial frames, alongside a wider index of available sources. Registration uses the survey rasters and
their supplied world files, with printed-grid checks for selected sheets. Metadata agreement establishes
a coherent coordinate transformation; the raster sampling interval is not an estimate of ground accuracy.
Historical map editions and photograph dates remain separate evidence.
The modern terrain and building layers provide context, not a replacement for history. The Lands De-
partment’s 5-metre terrain product documents photography from 2014–2015 and a March 2019 revision
[2]. A September 2026 building release is a different epoch [3]. Roof and base fields are approximate
elevations above Hong Kong Principal Datum. A roof elevation is not a height to be added to terrain,
and Hong Kong Principal Datum must be reconciled with the aviation height reference before clearance
calculations.


3.2 What has been recovered from the 1993 photographs

Two overlapping frames, A36142 and A36143, dated 2 November 1993, support an actual image-based
stereo reconstruction. The retained result has 1,318 sparse matched points and 171,307 filtered dense
surface points. Camera labels and scan-scale assumptions supply provisional intrinsics. Matched im-
age features determine relative geometry; manually interpreted ground features and survey-road controls
anchor a local metric transformation.
The vertical coordinate measures height relative to a fitted park/road plane. It is not an absolute surveyed
elevation or necessarily the height of a roof above that building’s own base. Two inspected roof features
have conditional relative-plane estimates of approximately 10.77 and 19.20 metres. They are different fea-
tures. Variation under selected focal-length and principal-point perturbations is a sensitivity experiment,
not a comprehensive error interval.

3.3 From surface evidence to aircraft clearance

Clearance concerns the aircraft’s swept volume and its tracking uncertainty. It cannot be established by
checking a single point against a visually plausible surface. Historical building identities, roof geometry,
terrain changes, occlusions, datum offsets and registration errors must be reconciled over the relevant
corridor. Unknown geometry must remain unknown rather than becoming an empty obstacle list.
A conservative observed-surface mesh now connects supported points into 225,371 triangles using
155,891 vertices. It retains holes and has 86,061 boundary edges; it is not watertight. An interpolation-
support correction removed 49 previously exported dense points before the geometry used in this draft
was frozen. The point counts and figure reflect that corrected delivery. Neither connected triangles nor
an attractive rendering establish complete building solids.
The useful intermediate output is an evidence-linked reconstruction in which each object or point records
its source date, coordinate meaning and support. Such a resource allows errors to be inspected and cor-
rected. The mathematical landing model can consume it only when the geometric assumptions required
by that model are justified.

Evidence status. Dated registered maps and conditional local stereo geometry are available. A complete
historical obstacle surface with independently defensible vertical accuracy and whole-aircraft clearance
is not established.

4 A coupled approach-to-stop model

4.1 Why the phases must be considered together

Turning, descending and slowing compete for time, distance and force. At a given airspeed, tighter turn-
ing generally raises lateral force demand. Extending the airborne path can change where the aircraft
reaches the runway and how much flare distance is needed. Touchdown speed then enters the stopping
law quadratically. An approach model that ends at threshold capture omits part of the constraint interac-
tion.
The current research model uses a coordinated three-dimensional point mass. Its aircraft identity is histori-
cally anchored, but its drag, lift, thrust, rate and braking limits are stipulated scenario parameters. It does
not include full attitude dynamics, sideslip, landing-gear mechanics or a calibrated wet-runway model.
These simplifications make an auditable benchmark possible; they also define the limit of its conclusions.

right and ℎ above runway level. For known constant wind w, define

Inverse dynamics maps a smooth position curve into required lift, bank and thrust. The model then tests
those demands against its operating limits, including bank-angle rate and thrust slew. A smooth curve
can fail those tests. Appendix A gives the equations and distinguishes forward numerical replay from

The baseline has zero nominal wind. Constant-wind inverse dynamics cannot be extended to time-varying
gusts simply by changing the position equation: air-relative acceleration then also involves wind acceler-
ation. The current mathematical result does not claim that extension.

5.2 Existence and an unresolved performance gap

Within the specified compact parameter box, a regularity argument bounds vertical speed and excludes
inverse-dynamics singularities on the positive-margin feasible domain. Continuity and compactness then
establish attainment of the primary maximum. An analogous argument establishes attainment of the
secondary minimum on the appropriate restricted set. These are existence results within the selected
polynomial family, not formulas for the optimizers.
A separate necessary-condition argument uses the minimum flare duration, the vertical-speed bound, the
minimum equivalent airspeed and the stopping constraint. It gives

6.3 The airport wind is not a single number

The ground-weather printout contains 15 irregular timestamps, five sensors and three A/B/C fields: 225
printed direction–speed pairs. The A field is a maximum one-second speed within a five-second window;
B and C are overlapping two- and ten-minute averages. At wind-system 03:35:30, the NW sensor shows
37 kt in A, 23 kt in B and 19 kt in C. At 03:38:00, NW shows 12 kt while Lei Yue Mun shows 37 kt in A.
These differences describe sensor location and aggregation window, not measured wind shear along the
aircraft’s path.
The ground-weather clock’s offset to the flight/ATC timeline is unidentified. No exact synchronized join
is imposed, and no particular ground gust is assigned as the cause of a flight excursion. The printout
was event-triggered and does not contain all recorded peaks. Interpolation would add assumptions, not
recover missing observations.


6.4 Annual station-weather context

The retained NOAA Integrated Surface Database archive contains 8,549 records for station identifiers
450070–99999 in 1993, including 24 records on 4 November [4, 5]. Decoding preserves source flags,
missing values and raw fields. A record containing missing wind is not converted to calm conditions, and
absence of a gust group is not interpreted as zero gust.
For descriptive comparison, the routine subset is fixed to FM-15 reports with source flag 4, exact UTC-
hour timestamps, normal-wind coding, present wind speed with quality code 1 or 5, and exclusion of rows
missing both temperature and visibility. It contains 8,360 observed records. This is a selected archive
subset, not a random sample of landings or a complete set of independent weather states.
The 03:00 and 04:00 UTC reports on 4 November both give wind from 070∘ true at 10.8 m/s, approx-
imately 21 knots. Their structured gust values are 17.5 and 20.6 m/s, respectively. Agreement of the
mean values with the CAD published mean is cross-source consistency; the sources may derive from
the same originating observations. Neither hourly value establishes the instantaneous three-dimensional
wind acting on the aircraft between reports.

7 Uncertainty, counterfactuals and the meaning of a better approach

7.1 Different errors require different evidence

Measurement error concerns observed inputs, such as positions or a plotted speed trace. Model discrep-
ancy concerns the difference between the chosen aircraft equations and real dynamics. Approximation
error concerns a compact rule’s difference from its reference computation. Tracking error concerns an air-
craft’s departure from the planned reference. An optimality gap concerns the distance between achieved
and best admissible objective values. These cannot be compressed into one numerical band without a
joint model and supporting evidence.



                                                                                13
Checkers with Runway 13                                                               Working white paper · v1


For example, a terrain product’s stated accuracy at a confidence level does not supply a worst-case ob-
stacle bound. An interval-arithmetic proof controls numerical enclosure under its assumptions, not the
correctness of the assumptions. A held-out horizontal map-control error does not independently calibrate
vertical roof error. Each number must retain the question it answers.

A prediction interval for an individual landing requires a justified conditional distribution for the outcome
and validation in the intended domain. It differs from a confidence interval for a mean or an estimated
coeﬀicient. Marginal intervals for touchdown and stopping do not automatically provide joint coverage,
and endpoint coverage does not establish clearance along an entire path.


7.3 The information available to a candidate matters

An offline optimizer may be given an entire future wind history. A pilot or causal controller cannot use
unobserved future disturbances. The distinction can be written as

           ∃𝜋 ∀𝑤 ∈ 𝒲 ∶ constraints hold          versus      ∀𝑤 ∈ 𝒲 ∃𝜋𝑤 ∶ constraints hold.              (10)

The second statement permits a different solution after each complete wind realization is known. It is
weaker than a single admissible policy that works across the disturbance set. A fair historical comparison
must specify the information set, response limitations and navigation aids as well as aircraft dynamics.
The primary counterfactual should keep aircraft, mass, entry state, runway, dated obstacles, braking, op-
erating limits and available information fixed. A modern-guidance comparison can be useful, but is a
separately labelled question. A candidate can only disprove the optimality of a particular baseline if both
are feasible for the same problem and its strict advantage exceeds relevant uncertainty.


8 Relation to existing research and contribution to Hong Kong

8.1 Where the research sits

Aircraft trajectory optimization, bounded-curvature planning and robust tracking are established subjects.
Wu and Chen characterize minimum-time paths for a Dubins airplane in steady wind; some candidate
families reduce to quadratic equations and others require transcendental root finding [6]. That reduced
setting is a valuable benchmark, not a complete descending landing with flare and stopping.


                                                     14
Checkers with Runway 13                                                            Working white paper · v1


Kim and coauthors study six-degree-of-freedom landing trajectories with runway alignment, obstacles
and constant wind using sequential convex programming [7]. That provides a substantially richer dynam-
ics comparator. FaSTrack separates a simplified planner from tracking dynamics and a tracking-error
bound [8]. Its demonstrations do not certify a Kai Tak airliner, but the distinction between planned ge-
ometry and trackable motion is directly relevant here.
The present paper does not claim novelty merely from applying optimization to Kai Tak. Its prospective
addition is the combination of a documented historical benchmark, explicit approximate trajectory rules,
transparent performance bounds and uncertainty-aware confrontation with records. Each element must
be evaluated against its own literature. The current screening is bounded rather than exhaustive, and the
comparative algorithms have not been reproduced in this version.


8.2 A useful historical research resource

The immediate public value lies in making a diﬀicult historical engineering problem inspectable. A dated
source catalogue and reconstruction can support aviation-history teaching. An explicit model can show
students why reaching the runway is not the same as completing a landing. A reproducible analysis can
help future researchers distinguish a missing input from a mathematical impossibility.
The intended public package would include the white paper, authored code, provenance records and ex-
planatory visualizations, subject to source rights. It would distinguish original materials from derived
artifacts and preserve gaps rather than inventing completeness. This version has not been publicly re-
leased, and no external adoption or societal impact is claimed. Potential uses are not evidence that those
uses have already occurred.
The work’s relevance to Hong Kong follows from its object: the geometry, records and engineering history
of a particular place. A generalizable mathematical method would be an additional contribution, not a
prerequisite for a careful historical dataset to be useful. Contemporary aviation applications would require
separate validation.


9 Limitations and the next version

The first draft has a mathematical candidate and historical evidence, but not yet a historical candidate
validated against that evidence. Its central remaining task is integration under defensible assumptions. It
must not close that gap by relabelling a synthetic path, replacing unknown historical obstacles with empty
space, or attaching arbitrary probability distributions to uncertain inputs.

10 Conclusion

Kai Tak can be studied as a coupled geometric and dynamical problem without assuming either that
its approach was uniquely optimal or that a better one must exist. This first version assembles a dated
evidence base, presents conditional historical 3D measurements, constructs an explicit synthetic approach-
to-stop candidate and separates its feasibility certificate from the unresolved optimum.
The candidate establishes that the stipulated reduced problem admits a complete landing trajectory. The
reconstruction establishes that historical geometry can be recovered locally from actual imagery. The
CAL605 benchmark shows how remaining runway and stopping-aid history can worsen the required brak-
ing even as speed falls, while the weather diagnostic places the case near the high-wind end of the declared
routine subset. None of these results alone resolves the historical landing problem.
The next version should connect them through a common, uncertainty-aware comparison. The contribu-
tion will be strongest where a clear statement survives that connection: a physical constraint identified, a
historical feature recovered, a model assumption rejected, or an alternative trajectory shown to improve a
defined objective under the same conditions. That is the standard by which this project should be judged.

B Restricted attainment and the surviving bound

B.1 Why a best member exists

The retained project proof first establishes |𝑣ℎ | < 14 m/s for both vertical-velocity polynomials across
the full parameter box. In this appendix 𝑣ℎ denotes vertical velocity; it is distinct from horizontal
speed. On the nonnegative-height feasible domain, the lower EAS bound implies total airspeed at least
135(1852/3600) m/s. Combined with the vertical-speed bound, this keeps horizontal speed above 68 m/s.
The positive load lower bound keeps lift away from zero, and the bank constraints retain the positive-up
lift branch.
Proposition 1 (Attainment in the nominal finite family). Under the fixed zero-wind scenario, parameter
box, continuous constraints, density and force model, corridor cloud and ground rule, the primary max-

Proof. The certified candidate makes the set with 𝜂 ≥ 0.0397 nonempty. Restrict 𝑞 to its compact box
