# Executive summary

A 747's turn over Kowloon looked extraordinary because several demanding tasks had to fit into the same limited space. The pilot had to follow instrument guidance, acquire visual references, turn towards the runway, manage height and speed, and arrive with room to stop. The famous 47 degrees describes a change between approach and runway directions, not the angle at which the wings had to bank.

*Checkers with Runway 13* explains these connections through historical photographs, dated maps, simple worked calculations and two complete mathematical landings. A moderate bank can produce a substantial direction change if time and distance permit. Touchdown speed matters disproportionately because braking distance grows with its square. A steady wind can become more crosswind-like as the aircraft turns, even when the atmosphere itself is unchanged.

The project's calm-air model lands and stops with approximately 255 metres of runway left. A separately optimized steady-wind case leaves approximately 553 metres. Each has its own computer-assisted check covering every instant of the modelled airborne manoeuvre and the stated stopping rule. The results are reproducible, but they describe assumed aircraft limits and synthetic entry conditions. They do not demonstrate that the paths would clear every historical building, that a real aircraft could track them in gusts, or that either path is near the best possible one.

The historical evidence makes these limits concrete. Photographs recover surfaces, but missing areas are not empty airspace. Approximate map elevations do not supply a complete city of known building heights. CAL605's published flight and weather records show why the actual sequence of braking-system states matters. The paper preserves the accident investigation's conclusion that touchdown was controllable and not causal; it does not claim that a different mathematical approach would have prevented the accident.

Version 3 provides 16 scientific figures, editable manuscript sources, technical derivations and a claims-and-evidence ledger. Its public value is to make the engineering understandable and the remaining research obligations inspectable. The next advances require better dated obstacle coverage, common coordinate references, calibrated aircraft and braking behaviour, and defensible tracking and wind uncertainty.
