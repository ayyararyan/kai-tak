Kai Tak's Runway 13 presented an unusually visible engineering problem: its instrument localiser and runway differed by 47 degrees, leaving a large aircraft to turn over Kowloon, descend, align and stop. This investigation follows that problem from the lift vector to the end of the runway, combining dated survey maps, aerial photogrammetry and the official CAL605 accident report with an explicit mathematical landing model. A worked turn shows how 15 degrees of bank at 78 m/s can accumulate the direction change in about 24.35 seconds. The complete calculation reveals a second constraint: touchdown commits the aircraft to delay and braking distances that depend strongly on ground speed. Constructed calm-air and steady-wind landings leave 254.68 m and 552.77 m respectively under the same assumed braking law; separate interval-arithmetic certificates verify their constraints continuously in time. Historical stereo images recover three-dimensional surface structure while exposing the limits of local control and incomplete coverage. CAL605's rollout record shows why reaching the runway and completing the landing are different engineering tasks. The trajectories use a reduced, uncalibrated aircraft model and synthetic approach geometry; the two optimized scenarios are distinct constructions, not a causal wind experiment or a reconstruction of the historical flight.
