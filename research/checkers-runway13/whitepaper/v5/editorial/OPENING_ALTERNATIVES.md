# Alternative openings and selection

Stage 2 working notes. These alternatives are editorial proposals, not historical reconstructions or first-person memories. None asserts a particular flight's unrecorded experience.

## A — The runway is not where the instruments point (selected)

An aircraft descending towards Kai Tak could be correctly established on its instrument approach and still be pointing away from the runway. The localiser led it towards Kowloon on a magnetic direction of 088 degrees. Runway 13 lay along 135 degrees. Between those two directions stood the problem that made the airport famous: a large passenger aircraft had to turn through roughly 47 degrees, descend, straighten its flight over the pavement and arrive with enough room to stop.

The setting made the geometry hard to ignore. Dense neighbourhoods crowded the landward side of the airport; hills rose beyond them; the runway reached out into Kowloon Bay. From the city, the final turn looked improbably close. On a plan, it becomes a question about space and time. How much bank is required to accumulate that change of direction? How much height and speed can be carried through it? And, after the aircraft reaches the runway, how much of the landing remains?

**Why this wins:** A concrete, counterintuitive opening immediately defines the engineering puzzle. It gets to the source-backed 088/135 relation without listing methods. The second paragraph locates the airport and turns spectacle into questions. It creates a natural next paragraph on instrument-to-visual transition. **Correction for final prose:** “pointing away” can be confused with air heading in wind; use “following a course that did not lead down the runway” or explicitly name the published localiser axis. Do not say each aircraft's air heading changed by exactly 47 degrees.

## B — A landing uses more runway than its touchdown

The part of a Kai Tak landing that drew the eye was the turn. A large aircraft crossed the roofs of Kowloon, rolled towards the runway and descended onto a strip of pavement extending into the bay. The less conspicuous part came afterwards. At a ground speed of about 78 metres per second, two seconds without braking would consume another 156 metres. Reaching the pavement had not finished the problem.

To understand that problem, begin before the turn. Runway 13's instrument approach and runway pointed in different directions: 088 and 135 degrees magnetic. Their 47-degree separation had to be negotiated in the final visual segment, while the aircraft descended and prepared to land.

**Strength:** Strong full-landing identity and a crisp numerical surprise. **Weakness:** Gives away the ground-phase revelation before the reader grasps the approach; the opening image risks sounding like an observed particular flight. Better material for the Act II–III transition.

## C — Two directions on a chart

On the approach chart, the two numbers are unremarkable: 088 and 135. Both are magnetic directions. Subtract one from the other and the difficulty of Runway 13 begins to appear.

At Kai Tak, the instrument localiser brought an aircraft towards Kowloon along the first direction; the runway occupied the second. The final visual segment had to join them. It did so in a city whose buildings, hills and water left the geometry exposed to everyone watching from below. The wings did not have to bank by 47 degrees. The aircraft had to accumulate a 47-degree change of direction, and that distinction is the beginning of the explanation.

**Strength:** Economical and mathematically crisp; excellent connection to the turn derivation. **Weakness:** Opens on numerals rather than a place and gives a central answer too soon. “Two numbers are unremarkable” is a mannered rhetorical device. Selected opening A is warmer and less stylized.

## D — The map cannot yet contain the answer

In the historical aerial photographs, Kai Tak is still connected to the city that once surrounded it. The runway, apron and nearby streets occupy the same image. Yet the photographs do not contain a flight path. To understand how an aircraft reached the pavement, the visible geography has to be joined to another kind of evidence: the directions on an approach chart, the mechanics of a banked turn and the distance required to stop.

**Strength:** Beautiful bridge between evidence and mathematics without pretending the reconstruction is complete. **Weakness:** Starts with method and the absence of a path, precisely the rhythm V5 must escape. Useful opening for Act IV after the model has created demand for historical geography.

## Selected final direction

Use A with the heading/track correction incorporated. Keep the first three paragraphs focused on the place, published directions and visual-transition requirement. Introduce no certificate counts, archive counts, optimizer terminology or repository claims before the reader has a physical question. A short source citation should attach to the bearings and a local qualification to the visual requirement; neither needs an apology sentence. The final introduction should ask the complete-landing question, then begin answering it with banked-turn mechanics.
